# Survey of World Models: Foundations, Recent Advances, and Open Problems

## TL;DR
- World models started as learned generative simulators for reinforcement learning, with the canonical early stack combining a VAE encoder/decoder, an MDN-RNN latent dynamics model, and a controller trained in imagination [1].
- Dreamer shifted the field toward latent imagination for long-horizon control, and DreamerV2 extended the approach with discrete latent representations and strong Atari results [2][3].
- Recent work increasingly treats world models as video-based simulators for embodied and interactive settings, with benchmarks such as WorldSimBench and WorldModelBench emphasizing perceptual realism, action usability, and physical adherence rather than only visual quality [4][5][6].
- Newer methods add geometry, explicit memory, and 3D state to improve long-horizon consistency, reflecting a move from short-horizon prediction toward persistent spatial simulation [7][8][9].
- Across the literature, evaluation remains unsettled: reconstruction and appearance scores often fail to measure whether a model supports planning, intervention, or policy improvement [10][5][11].

## Background
The modern world-model agenda began with learned environment simulators for control. The original World Models paper proposed compressing pixels into latent states and learning dynamics in that latent space, then training a controller inside a generated “dream” environment before transferring it back to the real task [1]. That formulation established the now-standard separation between perception, dynamics, and control. A follow-up reinforced the same modular logic while emphasizing policy evolution over the learned latent features [12].

A second foundational line came from latent imagination in Dreamer. Instead of planning directly in pixel space, Dreamer learned a latent dynamics model and backpropagated value gradients through imagined trajectories [2]. This moved world models closer to model-based RL as an efficiency tool for long-horizon decision-making rather than only as a simulator. The DeepMind Control Suite became an important benchmark family for these methods because it offered standardized continuous-control tasks with interpretable rewards and image observations [13]. Later, DreamerV2 showed that discrete latent world models could scale to Atari and reported human-level performance on the 55-game benchmark [3].

## Foundational World-Model Architectures and Benchmarks
The earliest influential architecture is the V-M-C decomposition in World Models: a VAE-style compressor for frame representation, an MDN-RNN for latent prediction, and a controller trained on top of the learned representation [1]. Its core claim was that a policy can be trained entirely in simulation generated by the world model and then transferred to the environment [1]. The paper’s CarRacing-v0 result is notable because it framed performance against the environment’s stated solving threshold and reported 906 ± 21 [1].

Recurrent World Models Facilitate Policy Evolution kept the same conceptual split but coupled the latent model to evolutionary search rather than gradient-based control [12]. This illustrates an early fork in the field: world models can serve either as differentiable planning substrates or as compact state representations for downstream black-box policy optimization [1][12].

Dreamer unified the latent-model idea with value-based imagination. Its central contribution was to use learned latent rollouts for control on 20 visual tasks from the DeepMind Control Suite [2]. The benchmark choice matters because these tasks include image observations, contact dynamics, sparse rewards, and 3D scenes, making them more demanding than small toy domains [2][13]. DreamerV2 then replaced continuous latents with discrete representations and extended evaluation to Atari 55, claiming human-level performance on that benchmark with a single GPU and a single environment instance [3].

## From Latent Control to Video World Simulators
Recent work broadens “world model” beyond control-centric latent dynamics and toward video generation models that behave like simulators. WorldSimBench frames this as a higher-capability stage of predictive models and splits evaluation into explicit perceptual evaluation and implicit manipulative evaluation [4]. Its three scenario families—open-ended embodied environments, autonomous driving, and robot manipulation—show that modern world models are expected to support both visual fidelity and downstream action utility [4].

Aether pushes this direction by combining 4D dynamic reconstruction, action-conditioned video prediction, and goal-conditioned visual planning [7]. The method uses camera pose trajectories as geometry-informed action spaces, which is particularly relevant for ego-view navigation and robotics [7]. Compared with earlier latent-control systems, this is a stronger attempt to bind the model to spatial structure rather than only to frame prediction.

WorldMem attacks a different failure mode: long-term inconsistency [8]. Its memory bank stores past frames together with pose and timestamp states, enabling reconstruction of previously seen scenes across large viewpoint and temporal gaps [8]. Likewise, Learning 3D Persistent Embodied World Models argues that video-based embodied world models are myopic without memory of unseen regions and introduces an explicit 3D memory map built from generated RGB-D video [9]. Both lines suggest that persistent world state, not just short-horizon next-frame prediction, is becoming central for embodied use [8][9].

## Evaluation, Comparisons, and Open Problems
A recurring problem is that benchmark scores do not necessarily reflect decision quality. The decision-making-centric position paper argues that visual plausibility is not the decisive criterion and that the real question is whether a model supports reliable interventional reasoning, planning, policy evaluation, and policy optimization under intervention and long-horizon rollout [10]. It also describes a mismatch between prediction/reconstruction metrics and control utility, which echoes the original model-based RL motivation but is now being revisited in the video-world-model era [10].

WorldModelBench makes the same critique from a benchmarking angle. It emphasizes instruction following, common sense, and physical adherence, and reports that general video-quality metrics correlate much less with physics adherence than with frame quality [5][6]. The benchmark also highlights that better visual scores do not imply a better world model [5][6]. WBench extends the evaluation problem to interactive, multi-turn settings, with dimensions including video quality, setting adherence, interaction adherence, consistency, and physics compliance [11]. Its reported diversity of scene types and control modalities underscores how incomplete many earlier benchmarks remain for interaction-heavy world models [11].

Overall, the field is converging on a broader definition of world modeling: not just predicting pixels, but maintaining persistent state, supporting controllable interventions, and enabling planning in embodied environments [4][7][8][9][10][11]. Recent benchmark work also extends this shift to embodied interaction and causal evaluation, as WoW reports a 14-billion-parameter generative world model trained on 2 million robot interaction trajectories and frames WoWBench around physical consistency and causal reasoning in video [14]. The unresolved questions are whether a single model can simultaneously optimize perceptual realism, physical fidelity, memory consistency, and decision utility; which metrics best predict downstream success; and how to benchmark open-ended interactive use without overfitting to superficial visual quality [10][5][11].
